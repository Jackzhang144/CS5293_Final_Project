Introduction

The Core Problem: Capability vs. Safety

Large language models are trained to be both capable and safe — but these two objectives can fundamentally conflict. When a model possesses strong capabilities in a harmful domain (e.g., synthesizing dangerous information), safety behaviors can be overridden by cleverly crafted inputs. This tension is the root cause of jailbreaking vulnerabilities.

⚔️
Competing Objectives
Modern LLMs are optimized for both capability and safety, but RLHF cannot guarantee safety when strong capabilities activate in harmful domains. The reward model itself can be gamed, creating exploitable gaps.
🎯
Mismatched Generalization
Safety training covers only a finite subset of all harmful requests. Semantically equivalent queries outside the training distribution evade detection — even simple rephrasing can bypass safeguards.
🌐
High-Stakes Deployment
LLMs are deployed in healthcare diagnostics, legal consultation, and autonomous systems. A successful jailbreak can enable harmful medical advice, private data exfiltration, weapons construction, and system manipulation.
📈
Democratization of Attacks
Open-weight models (LLaMA, Mistral) eliminate API-level safety guardrails. Automated attack tools (GCG, PAIR, AutoDAN) enable sophisticated attacks with modest budgets and no technical expertise required.
Key Research Question
How can we build LLMs that are both highly capable and robustly safe? Current alignment techniques are effective but fundamentally limited — understanding why requires examining both the attacks and the defenses in depth.
Background

LLM Safety Alignment: Methods and Failure Modes

Alignment Methods
🧠
RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
Fine-tunes a pretrained model using a reward model trained on human preference data. The policy model is optimized to maximize predicted reward. Effective for improving helpfulness, but the reward model can be gamed — the model learns to satisfy the reward signal in ways that circumvent safety guardrails.
📜
Constitutional AI (CAI)
Uses natural language principles (a "constitution") to guide model behavior through self-critique and revision. The model evaluates its own outputs against constitutional principles and revises harmful content. Reduces reliance on extensive human harm annotations while achieving competitive harmlessness.
Root Causes of Failure (Wei et al., 2023)

Competing Objectives

Models are trained to be both capable and safe, but capability and safety objectives can conflict. When a prompt activates a domain where the model has strong capabilities, safety behaviors may be overridden. The model's capability to provide harmful information directly undermines its safety training.

Mismatched Generalization

Safety training covers only a subset of all possible harmful requests. When an attacker formulates a request outside the training distribution — even if semantically equivalent to a seen harmful query — safety generalization may fail. A Base64-encoded harmful request achieves near-100% success against GPT-4, revealing that safety training coverage fundamentally lags behind capability coverage.

Background

Threat Model: Who Is the Adversary?

We consider an adversary who interacts with an LLM through its standard input interface (e.g., chat API or web UI). The adversary's objective is to cause the model to produce outputs that violate its safety guidelines — generating harmful content, disclosing private information, or bypassing usage policies.

🎯
Attack Objectives
Generation of harmful/illegal content (weapons synthesis, fraud guides); disclosure of private or sensitive information (training data memorization); bypassing usage policies for unauthorized use; manipulation of downstream systems that rely on LLM outputs for critical decisions.
🔓
Black-Box Setting
The adversary has no direct access to model weights or internal states. This reflects attacking commercial APIs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini). However, the adversary may have partial knowledge of the model's safety training procedure and general alignment methodology.
⚡
Attack Surface
Any motivated individual with internet access and a modest API budget. Attack automation (GCG, PAIR, AutoDAN) lowers the bar dramatically. Open-weight models enable offline attacks without any API constraints or costs.
Adversary Capability Spectrum
From casual users sharing jailbreak prompts on online forums (manual, no technical expertise) to sophisticated attackers using gradient-based optimization or auxiliary LLM agents for automated, targeted attacks. The barrier to entry for effective attacks is continuously decreasing.
Attack Taxonomy

Jailbreaking Attack Vectors

Prompt-Level

Direct Prompts & Role Adoption

Authority impersonation ("As a security researcher..."), hypothetical framing ("In a fictional scenario..."), and persona attacks like DAN (Do Anything Now). Shen et al. (CCS 2024) analyzed 6,387 real-world jailbreak prompts — role-playing strategies were among the most persistent and effective across model versions.

Encoding

Base64, Translation, Creative Formatting

Wei et al. (2023) demonstrated near-100% success rate against GPT-4 with Base64-encoded harmful requests. Safety training never encounters encoded variants — the model was trained to refuse direct harmful requests, but the encoding creates a distribution shift that bypasses safety generalization entirely.

Indirect Injection

Prompt Injection via External Data

Greshake et al. (AISec 2023) revealed that attackers can inject malicious instructions into webpages, documents, or emails that LLMs process autonomously. In RAG systems or LLM-integrated email clients, hidden instructions override the model's original system prompt — the attack originates from a seemingly trusted source.

Multi-Turn

Context Window Exploitation & Many-Shot Jailbreaking

Multi-turn attacks gradually escalate requests across exchanges — each message appears benign, with malicious intent revealed only in full context. Anthropic (2024) showed that filling context with many demonstrations of harmful behavior induces replication. Attack payload is distributed across the entire context window, evading per-message safety checks.

Automated

GCG, PAIR, AutoDAN — Automated Prompt Generation

Zou et al. (2023): GCG optimizes adversarial suffixes via gradient-based discrete optimization (~88% success on Vicuna-7B, transfers to GPT-4 at 47%). Chao et al. (2023): PAIR uses an attacker LLM with ~20 black-box queries (80%+ success). Liu et al. (ICLR 2024): AutoDAN uses genetic algorithms to generate stealthy, low-perplexity prompts that evade automated detectors.

Defense Methods

Three Lines of Defense

Proactive
Input Filtering
Examines prompts before they reach the LLM. Methods include: keyword matching to block known harmful patterns; semantic classifiers trained to detect malicious intent; perplexity filtering (flags high-perplexity inputs, a signature of adversarial construction); retokenization and paraphrase detection.

SmoothLLM (Robey et al., 2023): applies random character-level perturbations to input prompts. The key insight: adversarial suffixes are fragile — small random changes disrupt the adversarial pattern while leaving benign prompts unaffected. Reduces GCG success from >80% to <1%.
Reactive
Output Filtering
Applies safety checks to model responses before returning them to users. Catches successful jailbreaks that slip past input filters — a necessary safety net. Main trade-off: introduces inference latency and risks incorrectly blocking legitimate outputs (false positives).

Llama Guard (Inan et al., 2023): LLM-as-judge approach using a dedicated safety classifier with configurable taxonomy. RigorLLM (Yuan et al., 2024): ensemble framework combining energy-based models, k-NN, and minimax optimization. Diverse detection mechanisms make universally effective bypasses harder to develop.
Fundamental
Alignment-Based Defense
Addresses root cause — makes models inherently more resistant through improved training. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022): natural language principles guide self-critique and revision. Constitutional Classifiers (Anthropic, 2025): extends CAI principles to classifier training, blocking 95%+ of universal jailbreaks with only 0.38% false rejection increase on legitimate queries.

Limitation: alignment cannot cover all possible harmful requests — the generalization gap is fundamental.
Defense-in-Depth
No single defense is sufficient. Jain et al. (2023) show that single-layer defenses can be circumvented by adaptive attacks — an attacker who knows the defense mechanism can iteratively modify the jailbreak until it passes. A defense-in-depth approach combining multiple complementary techniques is essential.

Critical Insight — StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024)
Empty Jailbreak Phenomenon: Attacks that successfully evade automated detectors often produce outputs that are simultaneously nonsensical and non-harmful. This reveals that standard metrics like Attack Success Rate (ASR) are misleading — ASR measures evasion, not actual harm. Output quality assessment must complement ASR to capture true attack effectiveness.
Evaluation

Benchmarks: Measuring Progress in LLM Safety

🏋️
HarmBench (Mazeika et al., ICML 2024)
Standardized framework: 18 red-teaming methods across 33 target LLMs. Unified pipeline: attack → generate → classify. Key finding: adversarial training significantly improves robustness against automated attacks, though with measurable capability trade-offs on standard benchmarks. Breadth enables cross-model vulnerability comparisons.
🚫
StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024)
Reveals that existing benchmarks systematically overestimate attack effectiveness. Through human evaluation of "successful" jailbreaks, identifies the empty jailbreak phenomenon: many attacks produce rambling nonsense rather than genuinely useful harmful information. ASR metrics must be complemented with output quality assessment.
📊
JailbreakBench (Chao et al., NeurIPS 2024)
Open-source benchmark with 200 harmful behavior categories and a public leaderboard for reproducible research. Each category contains multiple prompts; models are evaluated on whether they refuse all prompts. Enables community-wide benchmarking, though reliance on automated safety classifiers inherits the limitations above.
⚠️
Benchmark Limitations
No "universal" attack succeeds across all models and behavior categories. Different models exhibit different vulnerability profiles — safety depends sensitively on specific alignment procedures, not just model scale. The co-evolutionary arms race means benchmark results are ephemeral; continuous updates are required.
Discussion

Open Problems & Future Directions

📊
Generalization Gap
The space of possible harmful requests is effectively unbounded, while safety training data is necessarily finite. Novel formulations are always ahead of known patterns — this gap is fundamental, not engineering. Defensive approaches relying on known attack patterns will always lag behind novel attack discovery.
📏
Evaluation Subjectivity
What constitutes "harmful" varies by context and culture. A medical advice chatbot may need different safety boundaries than a creative writing assistant. Automated metrics miss deployment-specific requirements; human evaluation is expensive and inconsistently applied.
⚖️
Safety-Utility Trade-off
Over-restrictive safety degrades utility by over-refusing legitimate queries; over-permissive models pose security risks. Quantifying and optimizing this trade-off principledly remains open. As models become more capable, the boundary of "safe and helpful" shifts — this trade-off is not stationary.
🔄
Adaptive Attackers
Sophisticated attackers adapt in closed loops: GCG → SmoothLLM → adapted GCG; PAIR → output filters → adapted PAIR. Static defenses will inevitably be circumvented — sustained, adaptive investment in defense research is necessary, mirroring the threat landscape in traditional cybersecurity.
Emerging Attack Surfaces
Vision-language models introduce harmful content encoded in images that text-only safety training does not cover. Agentic systems that execute code or interact with external tools can cause real-world harm beyond text generation. These emerging capabilities require dedicated, cross-modal defense mechanisms.
Conclusion

Key Takeaways

⚔️
An Asymmetric Arms Race
Attackers need only find one vulnerability; defenders must cover all paths. The bar for attackers is continuously lowered by automation tools (GCG, PAIR, AutoDAN) and open-weight models. Sustained investment in both offense and defense research is necessary — this is a long-term, co-evolutionary challenge.
📊
Metrics Must Evolve
ASR alone is insufficient — it measures evasion, not harm. The empty jailbreak phenomenon reveals that attacks evading automated detection often produce useless output. Output quality assessment must complement ASR; StrongREJECT's framework is a crucial step toward more honest evaluation.
🔬
Fundamental Limits of Alignment
The safety-utility trade-off reflects deep properties of how LLMs learn and generalize. Safety properties emerge from statistical patterns in training data rather than principled architectural guarantees. Achieving robust safety requires fundamental advances in alignment methodology, not just incremental improvements to existing defenses.

Achieving robust LLM safety will require fundamental advances in alignment methodology, more sophisticated evaluation frameworks that capture deployment-realistic risks, and theoretical understanding of the limits of safety guarantees. As LLMs are integrated into increasingly consequential applications, the urgency of this research agenda cannot be overstated.
